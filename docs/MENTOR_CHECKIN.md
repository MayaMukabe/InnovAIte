# Mentor check-in

Use these answers conversationally. Do not read them as a checklist.

## Can you understand our problem in one sentence?

Students who use AI for instant answers can lose the productive struggle needed to build reasoning, confidence, and learner agency.

The need is current: College Board reports that high-school students using generative AI for schoolwork increased from 79% to 84% between January and May 2025.

## Is our prototype proving the most important idea?

Yes. Brain Builder proves that an AI-powered learning experience can reward thinking before answers:

1. The student thinks and attempts the problem.
2. Coaching supports the process without revealing the answer.
3. Reviewed content—not a language model—owns correctness.
4. The student explains the strategy and sees that effort grow a Brain City.
5. A timed, no-hint Boss Battle checks whether learning transfers independently.

The clearest live “wow” moment is a correct Boss answer triggering animated damage. The deeper product proof is the complete **Think → Attempt → Guidance → Reflect → Grow** loop.

## Where might AI output be inaccurate, biased, unsafe, or misleading?

| Risk | Where it could appear | Product guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | A generated hint or explanation review could be wrong. | Reviewed question-bank answers own correctness; source-grounded material questions show evidence. |
| Bias | Writing feedback could favor particular vocabulary, dialects, ages, or English fluency. | Judge mathematical ideas separately from phrasing, allow alternate explanations, and test with diverse learners. |
| Safety | Failure messages or Boss dialogue could shame a child. | Keep language encouraging, avoid rankings that humiliate, and provide adjustable pacing. |
| Privacy | Notes, mistakes, and reflections can expose student information. | Minimize collection, process prototype uploads in memory, and do not claim the prototype is school-data ready. |
| Overreliance | Hints could become another answer shortcut. | Require an attempt first, use process-only hints, and provide no hints in the transfer-focused Boss mode. |
| Misleading claims | A polished prototype can look production-ready. | Clearly disclose current local storage and deterministic coaching; authentication, school controls, and production AI are roadmap work. |

## What should remain under human control?

The student controls the strategy, attempt, hint timing, explanation, retry, uploaded material, and whether progress is exported. Educators should control curriculum approval and review. AI may transform notes and coach the learning process, but it must not silently become the authority on correctness or student ability.

## What should we simplify before the final demo?

- Follow one learner, “Maya,” through one clear story.
- Show Brain City, one short learning interaction, and one Boss hit.
- Mention Material Studio and Manga Rewards briefly; keep them out of the core live path.
- Do not tour every district, setting, catalog entry, or backend route.
- Pre-stage the Boss Battle and keep a backup video ready.

## Evidence of meaningful human involvement

The team should be ready to explain its own decisions:

- Why growth is represented as a city.
- Why coaching is separated from correctness.
- Why Boss Battles remove hints.
- How effort, explanation, and transfer matter beyond accuracy.
- Which prototype tradeoffs were chosen for the hackathon.
- How the team tested the journey and what it learned.

Disclose AI assistance directly: AI tools supported brainstorming, implementation, visual iteration, testing, and documentation. The team owns the problem framing, product boundaries, interaction design, review decisions, and final presentation.
